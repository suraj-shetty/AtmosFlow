One build, three skies

The same app, in three weathers

Three recordings made the same afternoon against live Open-Meteo data — a clear night in San Francisco, cloud over Tokyo, a thunderstorm over Guangzhou — lined up on the moment each one launches. Nothing here is staged or seeded: the palette, the glyphs and the falling layers are whatever the sky was doing.

The fourteen skies

Every condition, day and night

These are the app's own golden files — the same images the test suite compares each build against, so this page cannot show the app looking like anything the tests disagree with.

Seven screens

The forecast, floating over it

A 96px hero temperature over the live sky, an hourly strip whose chips expand to their precipitation line, a seven-day list, a day detail with a chart that draws itself on, search, saved places, and settings.

Home-screen widgets

105 tiles, generated not drawn

Seven conditions down, five times of day across — dawn, morning, afternoon, evening, night — in three layouts, on WidgetKit and RemoteViews alike.

The design writes those tiles as inline CSS. Hand-transcribing thirty-five layer sets into Swift and Kotlin would be seventy chances to mistype an alpha, so the CSS is parsed once into a typed model and both catalogues are generated from it.

Neither platform can animate a widget, so every keyframe is sampled at one shared instant — 2.944s, chosen because it is 92% of the storm's flash cycle, and a widget that holds one picture for minutes should have its lightning in it.

Designed and built here

One project, three surfaces

The app, the widgets and the brand all come from one design of mine, made in Claude Designs, and each is generated from that design's own CSS rather than transcribed by hand. The brand generator is checked by rendering it and the design at 248px and diffing — the output is pixel-identical.
